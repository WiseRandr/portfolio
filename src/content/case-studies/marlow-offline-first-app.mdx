## Context

Marlow Navigation manages crews for ships around the world. Before a vessel can sail, its captain and crew must have the right documents signed and in order, and once at sea they can be away for more than a month with little or no connectivity.

The Marlow mobile app, built in React Native, puts what a seafarer needs for a voyage on their phone:

- **Sailing documents:** lists the documents each crew member needs for a given voyage, and reminds them of required actions.
- **Payslips:** available to view directly in the app.
- **Voyage agenda:** shows the route and the activities or actions required at each port.
- **Allotments:** lets crew members ask Marlow to send part of their pay to a beneficiary, such as family at home. The app submits the request; Marlow's services process it from there.
- **Crew admin workspace:** gives crew administrators a view of every crew member's compliance status: who still needs to sign a document, who is leaving which vessel and where, and more.

## The constraint: working with unreliable connectivity

Crews at sea can go long periods without internet, yet they still need their voyage documents and still need to act. Three other constraints shaped the work:

- **No single API.** The app depended on many separate Marlow services, with no single entry point for the client to call.
- **A wide range of devices.** Crew members use all kinds of phones, so every screen had to work across many screen sizes and device types.
- **Saved isn't current.** Anything shown offline is what the app saved when it was last online, so the app has to say so rather than present it as up to date.

## My role

I was a frontend engineer on the mobile team, working across the app through continuous releases:

- **Feature delivery:** built and shipped features release after release.
- **Offline features:** built the document downloads and the document screens, and the pending actions that hold a request made offline until it can be submitted.
- **API architecture:** proposed a middleware API between the app and Marlow's services, which the team built and adopted.
- **Collaboration:** worked closely with my teammates, Marlow's in-house API teams, the product owner, and QA testers.

## Opening the app: passcode first, then online or offline

Every time the app opens, the user verifies their passcode, on a screen a teammate built. The app then checks connectivity. Online, it fetches current information and triggers submission of any pending actions. Offline, the user can still get in after verifying the passcode and see saved content, with an indicator that it may not be the latest.

## Documents: what's saved, and saying so

Each document in the list has two actions, revealed by swiping the row from right to left: **Download** (or **Remove download** once a copy is saved) and **Share**.

<DocumentAccessDemo />

### Download on request, keep until removed

A document is available offline only if the user downloaded it while online. It then stays available until the user removes the download. Files arrive from the API base64-encoded, and the app keeps them on the device from there. The trade-off is that the user has to plan ahead: a document that wasn't downloaded before the ship lost connectivity can't be opened at sea.

### Tell the user when information is saved, not current

Offline, the app shows "Offline · Showing saved information", because the list may no longer match what Marlow has. Being explicit costs a little screen space, and makes crew less likely to act on a list they assume is current.

### Block what needs a connection, keep the screen usable

Downloading and sharing need a connection, even for a document that's already downloaded. Offline, they show a notification instead of failing, and the rest of the screen stays usable. Removing a download works offline, since it only touches the device.

## Allotment requests that wait for the next online start

A crew member picks a beneficiary from their existing list and enters an amount. Online, the request is submitted to Marlow's service; offline, it's kept on the device as a pending action, and it survives a full restart of the app.

<AllotmentRequestDemo />

In this app, pending submissions were triggered when the user reopened the app online, rather than immediately on reconnection or through an operating-system background task. After the passcode is verified, an app event triggers the Redux-Saga actions that submit the pending requests, and the app then clears the submitted requests from the device. A submitted request has reached Marlow's service for processing; it is not a completed bank transfer.

## One API in front of many services

Rather than have the app call each of Marlow's services directly, I proposed a middleware API that sits in between and talks to the services on the app's behalf. The team built and adopted it as the app's single entry point. This gave the mobile app one consistent interface and centralized integration with Marlow's services.

## Results

Qualitative outcomes; no usage or reliability measurements are claimed:

- **Voyage documents without connectivity:** downloaded documents open offline.
- **Clear about saved information:** offline screens say their content may be out of date.
- **Offline allotment requests are kept** as pending actions instead of failing, including across a full app restart.
- **One app for the voyage:** voyage documents, payslips, voyage information and allotment requests for crew, and crew-administration status for admins. Offline support differs by feature; this case study covers documents and allotment requests.

## Limitations

- **Documents must be downloaded in advance.** Anything not downloaded before losing connectivity is unavailable offline.
- **Saved information can become stale.** The offline indicator says so, but it can't make the data current.
- **Sharing needs a connection,** even for downloaded documents.
- **Pending requests wait for a reopening.** They aren't submitted until the app is reopened online.

## What I'd do differently

### Map cross-team dependencies before implementation

At first I underestimated how much of our frontend work depended on other teams, and some of it ended up waiting on upstream integration. Mapping those dependencies earlier, and agreeing API contracts before implementation, would have made planning more predictable.

Today I'd start each cross-team feature with:

- **A shared API contract** for the agreed behaviour.
- **Named owners** for each dependency, visible to every team.
- **Representative fixtures or mocks,** so frontend work can proceed in parallel.
- **Early integration checks** against the real services, since passing against mocks isn't the same as passing against the real API.
- **Clear readiness and acceptance criteria** for each piece of work.

### Treat documentation as the source of truth

Communication inside my own team came naturally, but as the work spread across teams, group chats and video calls stopped being enough. I made documentation a team practice, because the project's maintainability depended on it. In hindsight, what matters most across teams is writing down API expectations, the decisions behind them, who owns what, and what has changed.
